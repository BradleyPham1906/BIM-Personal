Putting the project on the earth

The project's model 0,0 sits at the site's latitude and longitude (Properties > Site > Location). To set it:

  • type the latitude and longitude there; or
  • run FINDADDRESS and search OpenStreetMap's Nominatim. It runs one search per press, as Nominatim asks. The place found is also the site's place for its standards (see Standards), in the same undo step.

TRUENORTH turns the model to true north.

The map

MAP steps through the basemaps:

  • off;
  • street map (Esri World Street Map);
  • satellite (Esri World Imagery);
  • your own tiles.

Each map's credit is shown on the drawing. Tiles are cached in the browser.

Property lines

  • PROPERTYLINE draws a property by its bearings and distances.
  • PROPERTYFROMSHAPE makes one from a drawn shape.

Setbacks follow the property.

Site context in one click

CONTEXT fetches what is around the site, in one undo step:

  • From OpenStreetMap (through Overpass): buildings, roads, water, green areas, trees, railways, airports, power lines and land use.
  • From AWS Terrain Tiles: the ground.

Buildings come in at their height and stand on the ground. Where OpenStreetMap has roof tags or building parts, they get them: see Buildings and LOD.

The rest comes in 3D too. Each keeps its line or outline, which you can select and snap to, and carries a surface:

KindIn 3D
RoadsA strip as wide as the width tag, or its lanes at 3.3 m each, or a width for its class. Coloured by use: car road, pedestrian zone, footway, cycleway or path.
BridgesA deck 0.8 m thick, 6 m up for each layer (at least one), on piers every 30 m.
TunnelsThe centre line only, named "Tunnel: ...". Nothing is drawn on the ground.
RailwaysRail, light rail, subway and narrow gauge 3.2 m wide; tram 2.6 m. A platform stands 1 m high.
AirportsRunways 45 m wide and taxiways 18 m, or as tagged. Aprons and helipads are paved. The aerodrome is its boundary.
TreesA trunk and a crown, as tall as the height tag, else 8 m. The crown is diameter_crown across, else a share of the height by the leaf_type and leaf_cycle tags (the Urban Tree Database's medium tree of that kind; 0.6 where the leaf type is not mapped). A needle-leaved tree is a cone. A tree row puts a tree about every 8 m, from end to end.
PowerPylons 25 m tall, or as tagged. The line is drawn 20 m up.

With On the ground ticked, every surface follows the terrain point by point. A change to that setting takes effect at the next CONTEXT. Select any of these to see its use, width, deck or height in Properties.

Properties > Site > Site Context sets:

  • the radius;
  • the kinds to get;
  • the Overpass server.

CONTEXTREMOVE takes it all away.

Site analysis

Site analysis is the Analyze tab (SITEANALYSIS opens it): one place, the analysis workspace, where the site is analysed, its analyses are read and the model's own analyses are run. It starts with one button.

Analyze site

Analyze site (ANALYZESITE) needs only where the site is: its latitude and longitude, or an address typed in the box beside the button. It does the rest from free open data, and lists each step as it goes:

  1. The site: the address found on OpenStreetMap (Nominatim), if the site is not placed yet.
  2. Context: the buildings, roads, water, green, trees, railways, land use and terrain, at least 300 m around. A smaller context radius is raised to 300 m, which the noise and the views need.
  3. Ground and water: the ground every 5 m for 150 m around the site, from the same free AWS Terrain Tiles, kept with the project in metres above sea level; and for a US site, FEMA's flood zones (the National Flood Hazard Layer). FEMA maps the US and its territories only.
  4. Climate and time zone: ten years of daily climate, a year of hourly wind and heat, air quality and earthquakes. The site's time zone comes with them, so the sun needs no typing: its name (America/New_York) and its standard time's UTC offset, whatever the season the climate was fetched in. An offset you gave is kept.
  5. Access and people: walk times to daily needs and transit; the streets within 800 m with what movement reads (speed, lanes, bike lanes, parking), the crossings' signals and stops, the doors on the lot, the car parks and garage entrances within 400 m, and each line's headway where it is mapped; and the census tract (US sites).
  6. Zoning: read from a council layer, where one is loaded in Data Layers and the property line is drawn.
  7. The analyses, drawn in the model (below).

Each step reads Done, Partly (some of its sources down, each named), Failed (with the reason) or Skipped (with why). What fails leaves the rest standing. The whole run is one undo step. Analyze site again fetches everything again, and keeps what you changed in the diagrams.

The run ends on the overview (below), with the site and its context and none of the analyses drawn yet.

The analysis workspace

The analyses are read one at a time, as archiMap and Forma show theirs. The Analyze tab is the workspace: its tab opens it, any other tab leaves it, and so do SITEWORKSPACE (also ANALYSISWORKSPACE) and TRAFFIC.

  • The bar over the view names the analysis in focus, with how many others are drawn under it; its cross leaves, back to the tab Analyze was opened from. What was drawn stays drawn when you leave.
  • On the left, one list: the site (its place, or a box for an address; its boundary, or how to draw one; Analyze site and how far a run has gone); Overview; the ten categories in the standard's order, each with its number, its findings counted and its red flags in red, and under it the analyses that answer it; Model last. Press an analysis in the model and it is drawn alone, with its key; tick others to draw them under it. Nothing opens in place: what you press is shown on the right. The search finds a category by its name, its question, its findings and the words of its analyses, their buttons and their settings.
  • On the right, what you picked, where Properties is while nothing is selected:
    • Overview: the site, the analyses run, the findings, the red flags first, the ten categories counted (each a link), what Analyze site fetched step by step, and Analyze site;
    • a category: its question, what was found, the analyses that answer it, its data (the context, the zoning, the climate and risk, the access and people);
    • an analysis in the model: what it shows, its settings (Traffic and people's simulation), Play and Stop for what moves, its chart where there is one, its key in full, what changed since it ran, what it found, the category it answers, the source and its date, the standards it used, and Run again, Select and Reset;
    • one of the model's analyses (colour by, areas, buildings, statistics, frame analysis, the survey, terrain, grading, rain, solar): its state, its settings and its buttons. Settings or Usages there opens the model's Properties, with a way back to the analysis above them.

Select an element and Properties shows it, as anywhere; a line picked while something is selected shows its panel and keeps the selection, for the buttons that act on it.

  • The view goes quiet in the app's own mode, light or dark (the rail's Appearance): the ground in the mode's colour, the map greyed and faded, the context's buildings, roads, water, green and terrain in the mode's greys, so only the analysis keeps its colours. Its words and its key take the mode's colours, the key at the view's bottom left. The drawing tools are put away until you leave.
  • The focus and the ticks are the layers' visibility under Layers > Site analysis, so Layers and the sheets show the same. Each change is one undo step, and they are kept with the project. Analyze site again keeps them.
  • On a phone the panel closes so the view stays in sight; the analyses are a row of chips under the bar, and Details opens what is picked. A category or one of the model's analyses picked in the list opens as the sheet, the list's drawer shut; an analysis put in focus shows the view.

Live and editable.

  • Select an element to move, turn, mirror, scale or delete it, or to change its colour, line, opacity or labels in Properties. An area (a slope class, a pond, a flood zone) is selected by clicking inside it, where nothing else is: the model and the lines and words over it come first. A selected area's edge is lit.
  • Slope and Aspect cover the whole ground, so they start with their layers off: Show on their row turns one on. Cycling stress and Walk times colour the same streets as the Street hierarchy, so they start off too. Land use, Figure-ground and Zoning around cover the ground as well, and start off.
  • Run the analysis again (in Properties, or on its row) lays it down again from what the project holds now. What you have not touched is refreshed. What you changed is kept, and marked when the analysis would now draw it differently. What you deleted stays deleted.
  • Reset to the analysis gives one element back. An analysis's Reset gives back its whole layer: what you deleted comes back, and what you changed or copied goes.
  • Properties says what each element shows, how it was worked out, and its source and date.
  • Diagram elements are not building elements. Schedules, quantities, the LOD checks and the exports pass them by.
  • A legend at the top right keys each analysis shown, with its source. Hide the legend turns it off. When the view is too small for every key, the sources go first, then the keys of the analyses you turned on longest ago, with a line saying how many more. On a phone there is none over the drawing: each analysis's row in Analyze carries its key.
  • The diagrams' words keep clear of the north arrow, the view cube and the legend, which are drawn over them: a word that would fall under one moves, or gives way.
  • Each analysis is also a row in its category, with Show or Hide, Run again, Select, Reset and its key: Compass in Location; Contours, Slope and Aspect in Landform; Water flow, Ponds and Flood zones in Water; Sun paths, Shadows and Winds in Climate; Noise in Environmental risk; Street hierarchy, Cycling stress, Transit, Walk times and Vehicle access in Access and circulation; Views and Daily needs in People and place; Land use and Figure-ground in Location and context; Green and trees in Ecology; Zoning around in Legal and regulatory.

How the noise is estimated. Open data gives a road's class and seldom its traffic, so:

  • the traffic is the class's typical daily flow, from FHWA's functional classes (urban where the area is built up), with 94% of it in the 18 hours;
  • the level 10 m from the kerb, and how it falls with distance, follow CRTN (1988), at a point 4 m up and no further than 300 m, where the method stops;
  • a building in between screens what is behind it, by ISO 9613-2's barrier term at 500 Hz, at most 20 dB;
  • the ground is taken as hard, and only the first building on each line counts.

So it errs loud. It is a screening estimate: measure where it matters.

How the ground and its water are worked out.

  • The ground. The project's own surface where it covers the site; else the ground Analyze site kept, when the site is still where it was fetched for; else the context's terrain, which is a point every 25 m, and says so. The ground under the site's origin is the model's 0.
  • Slope and aspect follow Horn (1981): each 5 m cell's fall across its eight neighbours, the nearest two counted twice. Slope is rise over run, as a percentage. 15% and 25% are the decision points: up to 15% building and roads are routine, 15 to 25% needs walls and engineered drainage, over 25% is seldom built on. Aspect is the way the ground falls, clockwise from true north; under 2% it is flat.
  • Contours are traced by marching squares between the grid's points, so each line passes where the ground is at its height. A saddle is read by the middle of its cell.
  • Water. Priority-Flood (Barnes, Lehman and Mulla, 2014) fills each hollow to the level its water could leave by, then D8 (O'Callaghan and Mark, 1984) sends each cell's water to its steepest neighbour. A flow line starts where 2,500 m2 or more drains through a cell, and its order is Strahler's: two of the same order make the next. A catchment of 4% or more of the ground is named, and the divide between two of them follows the edges between their cells, its steps eased to within a cell. A pond is where the filled ground stands 10 cm or more over the real one.
  • FEMA's zones are read as FEMA classes them: the floodway by its zone subtype, the 1% annual chance zones by their special flood hazard flag, shaded X (or B) as the 0.2%. Unshaded X, minimal hazard, is not drawn. A base flood elevation of -9999 means none was set; feet are also given in metres, on FEMA's vertical datum.

The ground is every 5 m, from elevation tiles: kerbs, swales, culverts and pipes are not in it. It is a screening of the land and its water, made before a survey and a drainage design decide.

How movement is worked out.

  • The streets are kept within 800 m of the lot with the tags movement reads; past 6,000 points the farthest go first, and the layer says how far it reaches. A street's class follows the usual crosswalk from OpenStreetMap's to the FHWA's functional classes. On a light map the higher classes are darker; on the dark plan, brighter. After changing the map, Run again on the row draws the other set.
  • Cycling stress follows Mekuria, Furth and Nixon's criteria (MTI report 11-19, 2012): a cycle track or path is LTS 1; a bike lane is rated by the lanes each way and the speed, beside parking or not, and with no parking beside it by its width where that is mapped; mixed traffic by the speed and the lanes, a residential street of under 3 lanes a level lower. A street is as stressful as its worst feature. Where a street maps no speed or lanes, its class's are assumed, as the noise analysis assumes them, and Properties says so. The speeds are read in rows: up to 25 mph (40 km/h), 30 mph (50 km/h), 35 mph (60 km/h), and over.
  • Crossings. Where a calm street (LTS 2 or less) meets a main street of another name, with no signal or all-way stop within 20 m, the crossing takes Furth, Mekuria and Nixon's 2016 table by the main street's speed and lanes. A median refuge is not counted, which errs stressful.
  • The low-stress reach is the streets the lot reaches at LTS 2 or less, a stressful crossing breaking the way, at most 25% further than the shortest ride. One-way streets are ridden both ways: a simplification.
  • PTAL is TfL's method: each line at its nearest stop within reach (8 minutes for a bus, 12 for rail, metro, light rail and tram), its walk, half its headway and a reliability allowance (2 minutes for a bus, 0.75 for rail) make its access time, and 30 over that its frequency at the door; per mode the best line counts in full and the others at half. The headways are OpenStreetMap's route intervals, so PTAL is given where every line within reach has one, as a floor where some do, and otherwise the layer says why there is none.
  • Daily needs and the nearest stop are reached by the walks (Access and people), traced back along the streets to the lot. The design guides' 400 m (to green space) and 800 m (to shops and a primary school) are said in Properties.
  • A vehicle entry is drawn where a driveway or service road crosses the lot line. With none, a provisional entry is marked on the lot's lowest street (an alley before a local street, an arterial last), as a parameter plan marks an entry until the design decides.

How the land is worked out. Each setting below is a record in Properties > Site > Standards (Land and green), and can be overridden there with a reason.

  • Land use reads each feature's tags by a crosswalk to the American Planning Association's Land-Based Classification Standards (LBCS), activity dimension, checked against LBCS's own descriptions: a hotel is residential (transient living), a port is movement, construction and waste are industrial, a cemetery or military land is institutional, a place of worship, a theatre or a station is assembly, a park is leisure, farmland or a managed forest natural resources, a wood or brownfield no activity. A building's own amenity, shop or office is read before its building value; an area's port before its industrial land use.
  • The mix is counted on a 5 m grid over the 400 m square: each cell takes a building's own use, else the smallest area over it, else grass's or a flowerbed's, which take the class of what they lie in. Each family's share is of the cells that say one, 1000 to 8000; the mix is Shannon's entropy over them divided by the log of the families counted (eight, the owner's choice): 1 an even balance, 0 one use. What says no activity, or nothing, is left out and said.
  • The colours are LBCS's hues, each stepped so that any two pass the colour-blindness checks (as on a map, where any two may touch), on the light map and on the dark plan; the textures, the outlines, the names and the key carry what colour alone cannot.
  • Density follows Spacematrix (Berghauser Pont and Haupt): GSI is the ground floors over the land, FSI the floor area over it, OSR (1 - GSI) / FSI, and L, FSI over GSI, the storeys on average. The land is the whole square, streets and all; each footprint is cut to it, less its courtyards. A building's storeys are its building:levels (less building:min_level for a part standing on another), else its height over 3 m, at least one. Spacematrix's types are not named: their bounds are in the book, which was not read.
  • 3-30-300 is measured as Browning et al. (2024) measure it, with what the app has:
    • 3 trees seen: the site's sight lines at eye height (as Views draws them), a tree counted where a line meets its crown first, within 100 m; a window view's stand-in;
    • 30% canopy: the mapped trees' crowns (diameter_crown, else 0.6 of the height) and the woods, on a 2 m grid, over a 500 m neighbourhood (the owner's choice: the rule fixes none), cut to the context's square and said so; ESA's land cover map, which the experts rate best, cannot be read from a page;
    • 300 m: the walk along the streets (Access and people) to a public green space of at least 0.5 ha, a park, garden, recreation ground or village green not mapped private: to a point of the network inside it, or to its edge from the nearest street within 60 m.
  • A council layer's colours are read as Esri's own code reads a layer's drawing info: its unique values (one field, or two or three joined), each value's solid fill and outline, the default for the rest, the layer's transparency. A hatched fill is not drawn; a layer with no colours, or one whose description cannot be read, is outlined and named in the app's.

The analyses in motion

The analyses are still until you press Play in the status bar, beside the view's own buttons. Pause keeps the moment; Play again goes on from it; Stop gives the still drawing back. The buttons show only when something can move. On a phone they come first in the bar, as icons. PLAY (also ANIMATE, PAUSE, MOTION) does the same as the button.

  • Marching dashes run along the water's flow lines and where the site drains, the view lines, the vehicle entries, the walk-time streets, the ways to daily needs, the bus and tram routes, the ways to the stops and the streets within a walk of them. A dashed line's own dashes move; a solid line gets lighter dashes moving over it. Lines with an arrowhead, and the routes, run the way they are drawn; the rest run away from the site.
  • The winds are drawn on along their direction, tail to tip, then held, then faded, one after another.
  • The distance rings grow outward from the site's centre, one wave leaving each ring as it passes it.
  • The layers come in one after another, in Analyze's order, each time you press Play from still.
  • The sun moves along its path from sunrise to sunset of the study's day (Properties > Site > Location), with its time beside it, and the model's shadow follows it, one shadow on that day. Or, chosen in the Sun paths' panel, through the year: the date steps a day at a time from 1 January to 31 December in 36 seconds at the study's time, the sun tracing the analemma, the day's own path, the shadow and the temperature's half-year following it. The sun study in plan (SUNSTUDY) follows the same clock. The project's own date and time do not change.

Each element's motion is a setting of its own: select it and choose Motion in Properties: Default (its analysis's), Still, or another its shape can take. Like any change of style, the analysis keeps it when it runs again.

The clock stops while the page is hidden. The app always opens still, and if the device asks for reduced motion while the analyses play, they pause. Sheets, prints and exports are drawn still.

Traffic and people

Traffic and people (Analyze, Access) simulates cars and people on the streets within 300 m of the lot. Press Simulate in its row, or type TRAFFIC (also TRAFFICSIM, PEDESTRIANS), which opens it in the analysis workspace, in focus: it plays on the same clock as the analyses in motion, so Pause keeps the moment and Stop gives the drawing back, in its panel as in the status bar. TRAFFICOFF turns it off. It needs Analyze site to have fetched the streets (Access and people).

  • The streets are the ones Access and people found, cut where they leave the 300 m. Each has its lanes (a mapped width caps them at 3.3 m a lane) and its speed.
  • The cars follow the one ahead by the Intelligent Driver Model, with the model's own standard car, and change lanes by MOBIL where there are two lanes or more. Each street brings the typical traffic of its class in the peak hour (FHWA's ranges at Florida's 9%), half each way, and each junction's turns are balanced to its streets' traffic (NCHRP 255).
  • Signals are simulated where OpenStreetMap maps them. Their timing is assumed, junction by junction: Webster's cycle from its traffic, held between 60 and 120 s, the yellow and the red clearance from the approach speed, and every green long enough for WALK and the crossing at 3.5 ft/s. Elsewhere the larger road has the way, a mapped stop stops its approach, and an all-way stop takes cars in the order they came.
  • The design's own trips come from its usages (Residential by its units, Office and Retail by their floor area, Hotel by its keys) at San Diego's rates (2003), in and out through the lot's driveway, or a provisional entry on its lowest street when none is mapped.
  • People walk between the cars and the lot's doors, and, at the census tract's shares, from the nearest stops and along the sidewalks from the edge. They cross only at junctions, at a signal while their green leaves time to finish, and cars wait for them.
  • The row sets the peak hour (AM or PM), the quality (Light, Normal or Detailed: how many cars and people at most; Auto reads the device) and the speed (1, 4 or 16 times). It says what the run assumes and what it has found.
  • Run lays the results on Layers > Site analysis - Traffic and people: each signal's assumed plan, the lot's entry with its queue, each door with its people an hour, and each junction's delay.

It is not a calibrated model: there are no counts, and each street carries its class's typical traffic. The signal plans are assumed: OpenStreetMap maps where a signal is, never its timing. In 3D the cars and people are drawn over the model.

The ten categories and the findings

Ten categories, always in this order:

  1. Location and context
  2. Legal and regulatory
  3. Landform
  4. Water
  5. Climate
  6. Ecology
  7. Environmental risk
  8. Access and circulation
  9. Utilities
  10. People and place

Each category holds its data, its analyses and its findings. The data writes the findings: Analyze site, and each fetch on its own. Each finding has a class (fact, opportunity, constraint or red flag), a severity, a source and a date. It is numbered by category: 7.1 is the first under Environmental risk. Red flags are listed first. Findings are to read; there is nothing to type.

They cover:

  • the location;
  • the built context (count, mean and tallest heights);
  • the property line's area;
  • the data layers and flood zones;
  • the terrain's height, relief and slope;
  • the site's slope from the ground around it: its mean, its steepest, how much is under 5% and how much 15% or more;
  • water and rain flow;
  • the site's drainage: which way it drains, how much land drains onto it, the ponds near it;
  • its FEMA flood zone, and the zones within 150 m: a red flag in the floodway, a constraint in a flood zone;
  • transit's PTAL, its floor, or why there is none;
  • cycling: the frontage streets' levels of stress and the low-stress reach;
  • vehicle access and parking: the driveways into the lot or a provisional entry, the car parks within 400 m and their spaces, and the parking along the frontage;
  • the day lengths at the solstices;
  • trees and green areas;
  • streets by use, bridges and tunnels;
  • railways, airports, overhead power and land use around;
  • and the climate, risk, zoning, access and people data, below.

A finding the data no longer supports goes. Everything is saved with the project.

Climate and risk

In Analyze, under 5. Climate, Get climate and risk (CLIMATEGET) fetches, for the site's latitude and longitude:

  • ten full years of daily weather and the last full year hour by hour from ERA5 reanalysis, via Open-Meteo;
  • 92 days of PM2.5 from CAMS, via Open-Meteo;
  • the earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 and above within 100 km since 1976 from the USGS.

All three sources are free and need no account. What is worked out is kept with the project, so the board opens offline. Each result also becomes a finding under Climate or Environmental risk.

Show in Presentation (CLIMATE) puts the Climate and risk board in the set and opens it. It shows the result as a professional site-analysis board.

The header gives the place, the Köppen–Geiger climate zone, the period and the date.

The indicators are:

  • climate zone;
  • mean temperature;
  • rainfall;
  • prevailing wind;
  • heating and cooling degree days;
  • solar energy;
  • outdoor comfort;
  • PM2.5, with a state against the WHO guideline;
  • earthquakes, with a state.

Nine figures, each titled with what it shows, with its source and a table:

  1. temperature (the mean daily high and low, and the band of 80% of real days), with rainfall below it on the same months;
  2. the wind rose for the year, with winter and summer beside it;
  3. every hour of the year as a heat map in named temperature bands;
  4. the sun path;
  5. heating and cooling degree days (base 18 °C);
  6. solar energy by month;
  7. the psychrometric chart with the comfort zone and the share of hours in it;
  8. the earthquakes by distance and direction, with the largest listed;
  9. daily PM2.5 against the WHO guideline and interim targets.

Notes on method and the sources close the board.

Hover any mark for its values, and press Tables for every number. The board follows the app's theme, prints on A3 landscape on white, and redraws its charts for a phone's width.

The climate is modelled at about 25 km, so a city's heat island or a valley's frost may differ. Confirm with a local station where it matters. Earthquakes are history, not a hazard model: for design, use the national seismic hazard map and code.

Weather and comfort (Ladybug)

Under 5. Climate, after the sun's rows, Weather for Ladybug and four analyses run Ladybug Tools' own code (AGPL-3.0), unchanged, in the browser. The first run loads its runtime, about 13 MB once, from the site; each of its files is checked against the SHA-256 the app pins before it is used.

The weather, one source at a time:

  • Open a weather file: an EPW, or a station's ZIP from climate.onebuilding.org, which holds its EPW and its DDY (ASHRAE's design days). The file is read in the browser and not uploaded. The project keeps where the hours came from, not the hours: after a reload, Run asks for the file again.
  • Get a year of hours: the last full year from Open-Meteo (ERA5) at the site, in its standard time. A site in a half-hour time zone gets its hours in UTC, and says so.

The analyses, each a row with Run, Show on the board and Add to a board:

  • Outdoor comfort (UTCI): every hour in UTCI's ten stress categories, in four conditions: in the sun or the shade, in the wind or in still air. The hours outside the formula's range are counted.
  • Psychrometric chart and strategies: every hour on the chart; the PMV comfort zone for summer and winter clothing; the hours each of Ladybug's five passive strategies adds (evaporative cooling, mass and night ventilation, occupant fans, capturing internal heat, passive solar heating).
  • Wind rose: the weather file's winds in 16 sectors, in the app's speed bands; calm is a speed of 0.
  • Design days: heating at 99.6% and 99%, cooling at 0.4%, 1% and 2%, from the station's DDY, or approximated from the one year, and said so.

Run all four runs them in turn. Their figures follow the Climate and risk board's own. The values they use (UTCI's scale and range, ASHRAE 55's comfort limit and still air, the clothing and the strategies' parameters, the design conditions) are in Standards, under Climate, with their sources; change one and each result that used it says it is out of date.

Zoning and yield

In Analyze, under 2. Legal, Enter zoning (ZONING) opens the district's controls:

  • the district, its permitted uses and the source (code, section, date);
  • the floor area ratio, the height limit, a storeys limit and the coverage limit;
  • a rule for each kind of side: the front (the street), the sides and the rear.

Each rule has a setback at the ground and, as the code gives them:

  • steps: "above 15 m, set back 6 m";
  • an angular plane rising from the lot line at a ratio: the front's sky exposure plane, or a side or rear daylight plane.

The front also takes the familiar street wall height and stepback above it. Pick the lot's front. The rear is the side facing it and the rest are sides; any side can be set by hand, for example a corner lot's second front.

From the layers reads a council zoning layer under the lot (see Data layers): the district and, where the layer has them, the FAR and the height. That covers NYC's MapPLUTO (its residential or commercial FAR, by the scheme's use) and an FSR and building-height layer, and a height given in feet is turned into metres. Each value is credited to its layer and field.

Build envelope (ENVELOPE) builds the zoning envelope as a translucent, locked solid on its own Zoning envelope layer. At every height, the envelope is the lot with each side moved in by its rule there, up to the height limit. Every change to the record rebuilds it.

From the envelope the app works out:

  • the floor plate at each storey's ceiling, capped by coverage;
  • the envelope's capacity;
  • the FAR's gross floor area;
  • the achievable area, the lesser of the two, and which one governs;
  • the net area, the units and the parking.

It also checks the design: GFA from the usages, the height, the ground coverage, and every element outside the envelope in plan or in height. Below the ground the envelope does not apply.

Show in Presentation (ZONINGBOARD) puts the Zoning and yield board in the set and opens it. It shows it all as a professional zoning and yield board:

  • indicators: lot, FAR, height, coverage, capacity, achievable GFA, units, parking, and the design, with a state;
  • the zoning analysis table: each control, what is permitted, what is proposed, and whether it complies, with an icon and a word;
  • two sections at true scale, front to rear and side to side, with every setback, step and plane dimensioned, after New York's ZD1 zoning diagram;
  • the rules by side: each kind of side's setback against height;
  • the lot plan, turned so the street is at the bottom;
  • the floor plates by storey;
  • the yield, from gross area to units and parking.

Hover, Tables, the theme, print and the phone work as on the Climate board. Each result is also a finding under Legal and regulatory, and every change is one undo step.

Check every control against the current code, and any overlay, variance or bonus, before relying on it. A lot whose setbacks would split it in two is worked out up to that height, and the board says so.

Access and people

In Analyze, under 8. Access, Get access and people (ACCESSGET) asks two free sources at once, with no account:

  • OpenStreetMap, through Overpass: every street and path around the site, the transit stops and the routes that serve them, and the places of daily needs;
  • the US Census Bureau: TIGERweb names the census tract, county and state at the site, and the American Community Survey's 5-year estimates give their people, with margins of error.

From the streets the app works out walk times from the lot, along the network at 80 m a minute (4.8 km/h), with steps at half that. The walk leaves the lot anywhere within 30 m of its line, so a lot with two frontages starts from both. Motorways, private roads and ways closed to people on foot are left out, unless a private way is tagged for walking. Sidewalks are walked but not drawn. A footbridge over the lot is walked, but it is not where the lot opens onto. With no property line, the walk starts at the site's point.

It works out:

  • the rings: the streets and paths reached within 5, 10 and 15 minutes, drawn as lines along the streets, as ArcGIS Network Analyst draws service areas;
  • daily needs: the walk to the nearest food shop, health care, school or library, park or playground, café, bank or post office, and community centre or place of worship, and how many of each lie within 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes. A park is reached at the nearest point of its edge. The kinds follow the 15-minute city; this is not a Walk Score;
  • transit: the stops by walk time, with the lines that serve them. A stop's two sides, and a station with its entrances, count as one. How often a line runs is read from its route's interval where OpenStreetMap maps one, for PTAL (Site analysis, Transit);
  • the frontage: each street the lot faces within 30 m, with the length of lot line it fronts, its class (arterial, collector, local, service), speed limit, lanes, sidewalks and cycleway;
  • connectivity: the intersections within 400 m of the lot, counted as LEED ND counts them. A junction leading only to dead ends is left out, and a divided road's crossing counts once. The density is set against LEED ND's thresholds (90 per square mile to qualify, 300 and 400 for points). It also works out how much longer the walks are than the straight line.

Analyze site draws the walk times in the model, on Layers, Site analysis (Walk times). The plan overlay of earlier versions is still there on request: WALKTIMES adds it as a Walk times layer under Analysis in Layers, and it follows the access data.

The people's figures are for the tract, its county and its state: population and density, age, households, income, renting, homes empty, households without a car, and how people get to work. Each comes with its 90% margin of error. A share's margin is worked out by the Census Bureau's formula for a derived proportion, and the tract differs from its county only where the difference is larger than the two margins together.

Show in Presentation (ACCESS) puts the Access and people board in the set and opens it. It shows it all as a professional access and people board:

  • indicators in two rows, with a state where a reference exists: daily needs, the nearest bus and rail, lines within 10 minutes, intersections; population, age, income, renting, no car;
  • the walk-time map, north up, with 400 and 800 m as the crow flies for comparison;
  • daily needs on one scale of minutes, with the nearest of each kind;
  • the street hierarchy within 400 m, line weight by class, with the frontage table;
  • transit by walk time, with each stop's lines;
  • connectivity against LEED ND, and route directness;
  • the tract against its county and state, each measure with its margin, and the key facts;
  • how people get to work, as 100% bars;
  • the age pyramid, men and women in five-year bands, with the county's outline.

Each result is also a finding under Access and circulation or People and place, and all of it is one undo step. Hover, Tables, the theme, print and the phone work as on the Climate board.

The census part covers US sites. Elsewhere the walk times still work, and the board says where the national statistics office publishes the equivalent. If a server refuses the request, or does not allow browser access, it is named; the app never routes around it.

Standards

Every standard, guideline and assumption an analysis applies is a record in the app's standards registry, never a number in its code. Properties > Site > Standards (STANDARDS, or CODES) shows them, area by area: transit and walking, roads and noise, cycling, air, flood and ground, buildings, structure, site context, land and green, climate.

The place and the date

Which edition is in force depends on where the site is, and when:

  • Find (or FINDADDRESS, or the address Analyze site looks up) sets the site's place from Nominatim's answer: the country, the region (a state, England), the district (a London borough) and the city, as ISO 3166-2 gives them. Set gives them by hand, as codes (US, US-PA, GB-WSM); Clear takes them away. If the site moves far from where its place was found, the group says so.
  • In force on is today, unless you give a date: a project held to the codes of an earlier date.

The edition in force is the one adopted most specifically for the place, on that date: the city's, then the district's, the region's, the country's, then an international one. Where two are adopted for the same place, the one adopted latest; an adoption's end date is the first day it no longer applies.

Where no edition is adopted for the place, a method the analyses need is still applied, and says applied outside its place, with where it is in force: TfL's PTAL is London's, the walking distances in Manual for Streets are England's and Wales's, and WHO's road noise guideline, which the noise layer gives for scale, is guidance for its European Region. The app's own assumptions (a storey's height where OpenStreetMap maps none, the slope classes) say they are the app's.

A topic, its values and its source

Each topic row shows its edition, how it applies here, what uses it, and the level its source was read at:

LevelMeans
PRead at its source.
SFrom a dependable secondary source; the gap is said.
UNot yet verified. Never applied.
AThe app's own assumption.

Values lists each value in words with its unit, and the source to its section, with a link. A topic of more than one edition can be chosen for the project in the list under it; By place and date goes back.

Overrides

Override sets a number for this project. It needs a reason, which every result that uses it shows. The value must be a number, more than zero (a percentage may be zero, a temperature anything), and in order where values are ordered: a walk to a bus stop shorter than to a station, a slope class below the next. A table changes only with a pack. Reset gives the standard's value back. Each change is one undo step.

What a result keeps

  • Each analysis drawn in the model keeps the standards it used. When one changes (an edition, a value, an override), its row in Analyze reads Out of date, says which and why, and Run again redraws it. Every row names the standards it uses.
  • The data Analyze site fetches keeps what it was worked out with: the walking speed of the walk times, the sizes given to what OpenStreetMap leaves unsized, the climate's degree-day base, calm and comfort band, WHO's levels. A finding drawn from that data states those values, and says when Analyze site again is needed to work it out with the values as they are now.
  • A change works the findings out again at once from the data kept, with no fetch: a threshold such as LEED's prerequisite, or a slope class's edge, is in the finding straight away.
  • A finding names the standards behind it, and an edition applied outside its place or a value overridden, with its reason.

In force here, not applied by the app yet

For the place, the codes in force that the app does not apply yet, each with its source and how it is had (free, free to read, or sold). For a Pennsylvania site: the Uniform Construction Code and the 2021 International Building Code it adopts, the 2010 ADA Standards, the MUTCD, PennDOT's Design Manual 4 and the AASHTO bridge specifications it adopts, and the FAA's airport design circular. The registry also lists editions not adopted for a place it knows (the 2024 IBC, ASHRAE 90.1-2025, ASCE/SEI 7-22, the Green Book), and, at level U with nothing claimed, those whose current edition could not be read (ACI 318, AISC 360, PennDOT's Design Manual 2).

Standards packs

A pack is a JSON file of up to 1 MB that adds editions and topics: a newer edition, or a place's own. Load a pack reads it and, before anything changes, shows its name, publisher, date and SHA-256, and for each topic the edition in force before and after and the values that change. Packs are unsigned: their origin is the file you chose. Apply or Cancel. Remove takes a pack away. Packs are kept with the project.

A pack never changes an edition already in the registry: a revision is a new edition. An edition of a topic the app applies must carry every value the app reads, of the same kind (a number, or a table).

A place key is * (international), a country (us), a region or district (us-pa, gb-wsm), or a city after its region (us-pa:philadelphia).

Trees in leaf. A deciduous tree is in leaf over the site's growing season: from the first six days in a row above 5 C to the first six days below 5 C after midsummer (ETCCDI's GSL), the median of the years of the site's climate (Analyze site, or Properties > Site > Climate). Out of its season the tree is bare, and only its trunk casts a shadow. Until the climate is fetched, a tree is in leaf all year, and its page says so.

Sun and shadows

SUNSTUDY shows the shadows and the sun path for the site's date and time. They are set in Properties > Site > Location. The sun's position follows NOAA's equations. A deciduous tree from Assets casts its trunk's shadow only on a date outside its growing season. Play in the status bar moves the sun through the study's day, or through the year (see The analyses in motion and The sun path).

The sun path

Sun paths, in focus in Analyze, draws the sky over the site as a dome of the compass ring's radius. A sun at altitude α and azimuth A (clockwise from true north) is the point X = R cos α sin A, Y = R sin α, Z = R cos α cos A from the site's centre, Y up and Z to true north, by NOAA's solar position equations (held to NREL's Solar Position Algorithm within 0.003°).

  • On the dome: the paths of 21 June, of 20 March and 22 September, and of 21 December, a dot an hour; the day studied, unless it is within three days of one of them; the sun now, its ray from the centre, the drop to the ground, its azimuth's arc on the ground from true north and its altitude's arc, named; the sun at the study's time every second day of the year; and the temperature where the sun passes: the band between the winter and summer paths by the hour and by the month, each cell the mean temperature at its hour over its days, in the climate board's colours. The band shows the half-year holding the date: 21 December to 21 June, or 21 June to 21 December (each place in it is passed twice a year, in different weather).
  • They follow the place, the time zone, the date and the time as you change them, with no run and no undo step of their own. One you coloured keeps its colour; one you moved stays where you put it.
  • The words: each hour beside its own dot; no word over a dot; a path's name on its path between two hours, where there is room.

The panel (Analyze, Sun paths):

  • The sun now: the place, the day, the time with its UTC offset and time zone, daylight saving, the altitude and azimuth, its point on the dome by the formula, the day's sunrise and sunset with their bearings and length, and the temperature typical of that hour.
  • Try it: sliders for the latitude and the longitude try another place, never saved (its clock by the longitude; Back to the site's place ends it, as leaving Analyze does); the date and the time are the study's own, the view following as they move and one undo step when let go.
  • Play: through the day or through the year.
  • The year's turning points: the summer solstice, the equinoxes and the winter solstice for the site's hemisphere, each with sunrise and sunset and their bearings, the noon altitude, the day's length and the shadow a 10 m building casts at noon.
  • Diagrams: the sky dome in plan, and the sun's height through the day, each with the sun now; and the temperature's eleven bands.

Daylight saving (Properties > Site > Location, or the panel): Off, standard time all year (the default, as Ladybug draws); Auto, each date's own offset from the time zone, so June's hours are by daylight saving time where the site keeps it.
